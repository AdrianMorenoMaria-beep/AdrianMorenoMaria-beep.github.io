About

I am a PhD candidate in Applied Economics at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona. I work in industrial organization and competition economics, with a focus on digital platforms, e-commerce and demand estimation. In autumn 2025 I was a visiting student at the University of Cambridge.

My job market paper studies what followed the European Commission’s 2017 Google Shopping decision. The Commission left the design of compliance to Google, and the results page Google built in response did not restore the visibility of rival comparison shopping services. A related project uses Amazon’s 2025 exit from Google Shopping to show that competition in one advertising format changes firms’ entry into another. A third paper measures what online-only retailers add to competition and welfare in a market traditionally served by click-and-mortar stores.

Alongside the PhD I have worked as a research assistant at the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre, on the economics of digital technologies, and I am currently a research assistant at the Institut d’Economia de Barcelona.

The House Always Wins: Self-Preferencing After Google Shopping

with Néstor Duch-Brown (Joint Research Centre, European Commission)

Self-preferencing by large digital platforms has become a central concern for antitrust authorities and policymakers. We study one of the earliest and most prominent cases of self-preferencing, the European Commission’s 2017 Google Shopping decision. Google was accused of systematically favoring its own comparison shopping service in search results while downgrading rival comparison shopping websites in its search ranking. The Commission prohibited this conduct but left Google discretion over how to comply: following the €2.42 billion fine, Google redesigned its search results page (SERP), reconfiguring the Shopping Unit to let third-party comparison shopping services (CSS) participate in its advertising system. Because the decision constrained Google’s conduct without prescribing the design of the remedy, what can be causally identified is the effect of Google’s redesign itself, rather than the effect of the decision in the abstract. Using web traffic data, we estimate the effect of this redesign on traffic to comparison shopping websites, comparing European countries where it was implemented with non-European markets where no equivalent design change occurred. Event-study evidence shows that organic traffic to comparison shopping websites in Europe declined further relative to non-European control markets after the redesign, while advertising-related traffic does not significantly increase. Notably, smaller CSS platforms experienced disproportionately larger losses in organic traffic, while larger competitors managed to sustain their visibility. These results suggest that Google’s redesign, while formally compliant, did not restore the visibility of rival CSS, an unintended consequence consistent with a remedy that regulators did not sufficiently monitor or constrain at the level of interface design. While previous work has discussed potential limitations of Google’s compliance mechanism, our paper provides the first causal empirical evaluation of its effects.

From Brick to Click: The Competitive and Welfare Effects of Digital Retail

with Néstor Duch-Brown (Joint Research Centre, European Commission)

Using panel data on vacuum cleaner sales across Belgium, Germany, and Slovakia from 2015 to 2019, we estimate a random coefficients demand model to quantify the competitive and welfare role of the online-only channel in a market traditionally served by click-and-mortar retailers. We differentiate between click-and-mortar retailers, including technological superstores, small retailers, and mass merchandisers, and online-only retailers, with marketplaces playing a significant role. Removing the online-only channel reduces consumer surplus by 12–17% and producer surplus by 7–10%. The online-only channel disciplines prices through stronger competition, benefiting consumers, while also expanding the overall market by reaching consumers who would otherwise not purchase at all, benefiting producers as well. When the online-only option is unavailable, consumers mainly shift toward retailers that replicate key features of online shopping, wide assortment, price transparency, and immediate product availability, rather than toward smaller, service-oriented stores. Our results highlight the importance of policies that sustain competition in the online environment, as they ensure that the welfare gains from e-commerce are shared across the market.

Work in progressOctober 2026

The Rival Next Door: Competition Across Search Advertising Formats

Digital advertising platforms increasingly combine multiple advertising formats within the same search environment, yet firms’ participation decisions are typically studied within individual formats. We examine whether competition in one advertising format affects firms’ incentives to participate in another. Because product ads and text ads appear on the same results page and compete for the same consumers’ attention, the advertisers shown in one format can change the expected return to advertising in the other. We exploit Amazon’s exit from Google Shopping in July 2025 to identify how a large change in the competitive environment of product advertising affects entry into text search advertising. We find cross-format spillovers: text-ad entry rises after the exit in the keywords where Amazon had a more frequent presence in Shopping, by about 15% of the mean entry rate per standard deviation of exposure, with the response concentrated among manufacturers. We then estimate a structural model of keyword entry with advertiser-level traffic and cost per click to quantify how competitive conditions across formats shape entry incentives. Counterfactual simulations show that, had Amazon remained in Shopping, manufacturers’ participation in text advertising would have been about 16% lower. Our results show that firms’ advertising decisions respond to competition beyond the format in which they participate, so that changes in platform design or market structure can generate competitive effects across advertising formats.
